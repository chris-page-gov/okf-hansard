Turn 166 · Speaker unlabelled

My Amendment 100 is very simple. When local authorities and integrated care boards draw up neighbourhood health plans, it would ask them to take reasonable steps to reduce health inequalities arising from the wider determinants of health and to involve meaningfully the communities that face the greatest barriers to taking part. In parts of south London, where I have worked for my entire career, children living a few streets apart can expect very different life experiences—not because of the care we give in general practice, which I hope is the same for all families, but because of the homes they live in, the air they breathe, the food their families can afford and the money that comes in each month.

Turn 167 · Speaker unlabelled

Every clinician sees this: the child whose asthma will not settle because the flat is damp; the man whose blood pressure rises because of his debts; the mother who knows what a healthy diet looks like but cannot afford it. Up to a fifth of my time in the consulting room is spent on problems that are not clinical at all. I can prescribe an inhaler; I cannot prescribe a dry home. These are health inequalities. The NHS Health Economics Unit estimates that 80% of a person’s health outcomes are shaped by factors outside the health service. The Government’s ambition for a neighbourhood health service is to move from treating sickness to preventing it. But a plan that looks only at services will treat the consequences of poverty, poor housing and polluted air while leaving their causes untouched. This amendment puts those causes on the table.

Turn 168 · Speaker unlabelled

The second part of the amendment is about who is in the room. Traditional consultation, which I have been privy to many times as a leader, usually happens through surveys, public meetings and online fora. It reaches people who are already heard. It rarely reaches those who are working two jobs, are caring for relatives or, with good reason, do not trust the authorities that are asking them the question. People from racially minoritised communities are twice as likely to need debt advice as the population as a whole. If they are not involved in shaping a plan, the plan will not work for them. Meaningful involvement means going to where people are—their schools, faith groups and community organisations—and listening before decisions are made, not after.
