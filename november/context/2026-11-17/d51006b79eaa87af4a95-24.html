Turn 185 · Florence Eshalomi

Clause 32 ensures that the chapter 1 duties work coherently alongside existing statutory and contractual routes for remediating external walls. We need clear responsibilities to secure remediation, not overlapping duties or competing enforcement action where another effective route is already in place. The clause therefore disapplies chapter 1 duties in certain circumstances. Importantly, it does not let responsible entities off the hook for earlier non-compliance or prevent regulators from taking enforcement action under other regimes. Clause 32 disapplies the chapter 1 duties where a section 24 manager has been appointed under the Landlord and Tenant Act 1987 and becomes liable to carry out the functions of an accountable person under proposed new section 24ZZA(2) of that Act. It also disapplies the duties where a higher-risk building regime special measures manager has been appointed …

Turn 186 · Speaker unlabelled

The duties are also disapplied where a remediation management order is made under schedule 1. In those cases, a remediation authority has been appointed by the tribunal to carry out works where the duty holder has failed. Remediation is therefore already being secured through the remediation management order, so continuing to apply chapter 1 duties on the responsible entity would duplicate rather than advance the remediation of the building. Those managers are appointed to step in where there have been management failures at the building.

Given that, clause 32 does not erase earlier wrongdoing. Where a responsible entity failed to comply with its duties under chapter 1 before the manager or remediation authority was appointed, it can still be prosecuted or fined for that earlier breach. The exemption protects the manager who has stepped in; it does not remove liability for offences already committed.

Turn 188 · Speaker unlabelled

The duties are also disapplied where external wall remediation is required and is carried out under specified notices or orders under the Housing Act 2004, the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005 or the Building Safety Act 2022. Regulators retain their powers to enforce those notices and orders, including to take punitive action where those are not complied with. The exemption simply avoids duplicating enforcement under chapter 1 where remediation is already being secured through another statutory route.

Turn 189 · Speaker unlabelled

The same principle applies for buildings where a developer has committed under a self-remediation contract to remediating the external walls. Major developers are already subject to clear remediation obligations through their contracts and the responsible actors scheme, which are backed by powerful commercial sanctions. Developers that fail to meet their obligations may face legal action for breach of contract, or prohibitions that effectively prevent them from developing new homes in England.

Turn 190 · Speaker unlabelled

Those sanctions can have substantial financial consequences, directly affecting the ability to do business and a developer’s bottom line. That provides a powerful commercial deterrent and sanction, at least comparable to, if not going further than, the legal duty to remediate. Developers are therefore not being left without consequences for non-compliance. They are already subject to a robust, commercially significant enforcement regime, and part 1 of the Bill introduces strong enforcement incentives for buildings outside those arrangements. I therefore commend clause 32 to the Committee.
