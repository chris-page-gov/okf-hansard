Turn 89 · Speaker unlabelled

Finally, I turn to Amendment 276 on a specialist detransition service, as recommended by the Cass review. It would require a specialist detransition service within two years, separate from gender services. The service must be open to everyone, wherever they were treated: the NHS, elsewhere in the UK, abroad or online. It must provide psychological, medical, endocrine and surgical care, including for the physical effects of previous treatment. With consent, it would record why people detransition and any concerns about assessment, consent or safeguarding. It would also anonymously report findings to Parliament every year, including the lessons for commissioning and regulation.

Turn 90 · Speaker unlabelled

This amendment is needed because, despite growing numbers of detransitioners and desisters, there is no government-recommended care pathway for detransition anywhere in the UK. Both the Cass review and the Levy review recommended one. The Government have proceeded with a puberty blocker trial for trans-identifying children, yet have not established a service to support those who decide to desist. While NHS England began engagement in April 2025 and consulted stakeholders in October 2025, nothing is yet in place. This is not acceptable.

Turn 92 · Speaker unlabelled

Clinics do not record detransition rates. Around 70% of patients disengage from adult services, and no one knows why. In a survey of 100 detransitioners, only one-quarter told the clinic. Dr Stella O’Malley, whose organisation, Genspect, works with detransitioners, is confident that UK numbers are “in their thousands”. What do they tell us? More than half felt that they had been inadequately evaluated before transition. Most say their distress turns out to stem from something else, such as trauma or a mental health condition that was not properly explored. That is why detransitioners need a special clinical service run by a skilled team, including psychiatrists who understand trauma and multifaceted complications, and that service must be separate.

Turn 93 · Speaker unlabelled

The Cass review found that people who regret their transition may be reluctant to return to the service that treated them. Dr O’Malley hears the same: that detransitioners are mistrustful of doctors, feeling that their treatment was an experiment, and that trying to go back would just be another, new experiment. In the absence of a national service, support has been left to the voluntary sector. Genspect’s Beyond Trans is perhaps the most comprehensive international support platform for detransitioners, offering them psychological help online. Michael Kerr, who I mentioned a moment ago, has founded Detransition Pathway UK to give others the help he could not find. He asks for three things: an NHS pathway, proper data on how many people detransition and funding support. That is what this amendment would provide.

Regret can take years to emerge, typically eight to 10 years after surgery. The large cohort over the last decade is only now reaching that point, so the numbers needing help are very likely to rise. The service should be in place before they do and be open to people of all ages.

Turn 95 · Speaker unlabelled

This is personal for me. My mum died because of the infected blood scandal, and I know what it means for a family when the health service does not look after them, does not ask and does not tell. The Infected Blood Inquiry found that the truth had been hidden and the disaster was “not an accident”. In a similar vein, at the Post Office, an institution insisted that its system was sound and would not look at the evidence that it was not. In both cases, the people harmed were told nothing was wrong. The Government looked the other way. It took campaigners and journalists, not official oversight, to bring the truth out. In both cases, the taxpayer is now having to pay the price.

Turn 96 · Speaker unlabelled

The warning signs are the same: no outcome data, a refusal to share what exists and staff who are afraid to speak. The first duty of medicine is to do no harm. These services have altered young people’s bodies irreversibly and at public expense, and they cannot tell us whether they have kept that duty. Transparency and accountability should not be too much to ask of services spending tens of millions of pounds of taxpayers’ money on irreversible interventions. I beg to move.
