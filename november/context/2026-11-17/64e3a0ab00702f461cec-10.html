Turn 73 · Speaker unlabelled

I note a cross-reference with the earlier amendment from the noble Baroness, Lady Barker. This amendment also recognises the contributions of voluntary and community organisations. Those organisations often have a very detailed understanding of the communities they serve, including the people who face the greatest barriers in accessing healthcare. They should be partners in planning, not simply organisations brought in at the end of the commissioning process. I note that this amendment would also require ICBs to report annually on how they have fulfilled this duty.

Turn 75 · Speaker unlabelled

I come now to Clause 24, which the noble Lord, Lord Hunt, has already spoken about. It removes the existing requirement for health and well-being boards to prepare and publish a joint local health and well-being strategy, replacing it with a new system of neighbourhood health plans. On the surface, that sounds good, local and in line with our current Prime Minister’s direction of travel. I understand the Government’s arguments that local systems need flexibility and that planning arrangements should not become an exercise in producing paperwork. But there is a difference between removing unnecessary paperwork and removing statutory expectations that encourage organisations to work together. That is why I have tabled this clause stand part notice.

Turn 76 · Speaker unlabelled

The existing strategies provide a framework for local authorities, the NHS and other partners to assess local needs and agree how those needs should be met. They provide a way of looking beyond the immediate pressures on individual organisations and considering the health of the whole population. The Government say that neighbourhood health plans will provide a more effective alternative, but a plan is not the same as a duty to co-operate. Organisations can produce a neighbourhood plan without necessarily making the commissioning decisions needed to deliver it.

Turn 77 · Speaker unlabelled

There is a wider concern here. Clause 22 removes requirements for joint forward plans, Clause 23 abolishes integrated care partnerships and their strategies, and Clause 24 removes the requirement for joint local health and well-being strategies. Taken together, these changes could make it much harder for health services to work together, just when the Government say they want them to work more closely. We should be moving away from the fragmentation created by market-based approaches to healthcare, not making it easier for that fragmentation to continue.

Turn 78 · Speaker unlabelled

As noble Lords are aware, local organisations already face considerable pressures. Local authorities are dealing with severe financial constraints. NHS providers are managing waiting lists, workforce shortages and increasing demand. Voluntary organisations are trying to maintain essential services on limited, insecure and all too often short-term funding. In these circumstances, co-operation cannot simply be left to good will. It needs leadership and clear responsibilities.

Turn 79 · Speaker unlabelled

There is also the question of who gets to shape these plans. A genuinely bottom-up approach must mean more than asking local people their views. It must mean that local knowledge, the experience of front-line workers and the expertise of community organisations can be part of decisions about how services are commissioned and delivered. We need that statutory foundation. The Government cannot claim to want an integrated NHS while removing the structures that support collaboration. Nor can they claim to be serious about integration while failing to provide a clear duty on those making commissioning decisions. We need more than plans promising joined-up care. We need a health service that is organised to deliver it.
