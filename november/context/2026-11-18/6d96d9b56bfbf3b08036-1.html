Turn 4 · The Attorney General

I gently refer the hon. Gentleman back to the Law Officers’ convention. We are proud of the role that Britain played in abolishing the slave trade, but we should also acknowledge the harms done when Britain was one of the largest participants in the slave trade. The Prime Minister is clear that this is morally the right thing to do. He has also made it clear that he does not believe that financial reparations are the right answer, but it is absolutely right to commit to a structured, time-bound, inclusive and good-faith dialogue on the transatlantic slave trade.

Turn 6 · The Attorney General

I thank my hon. Friend and constituency neighbour for her question. We both have a large Caribbean diaspora in our constituencies and are proud of the role they have played and continue to play in our communities. If we are to meet the challenges of the future, we need to be ready to face up to the past. That is why the Prime Minister has committed to establishing dialogue on the transatlantic slave trade. This will allow us to discuss its history and ongoing legacy, including the question of a formal apology, to which we are open. It is morally the right thing to do, and it also helps us to build relationships with our trusted partners.

Turn 8 · Jerome Mayhew

In his attempt to fend off the demands to pay reparations for historical slavery, not least from the Back Benchers behind him, the Prime Minister has proposed to work towards an apology for Britain’s historical role in slavery, together with a £40 million infrastructure payment to Caribbean nations. This would be, of course, an apology for something that no one alive is responsible for. Can the Attorney General confirm that this apology approach, linking historical acts with modern Britain, will not actually serve to increase the risk of claims for reparation payments rather than reduce them?
