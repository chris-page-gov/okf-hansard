Another example is consumer credit time limits—this was in relation to an affordability complaint concerning an overdraft opened in 1998. The Financial Ombudsman Service found that, in allowing the continued use of the overdraft and agreeing to increase it, the firm had created an unfair relationship under the Consumer Credit Act 1974. It took the view that, where a customer’s credit agreement is still live or ended less than six years before the complaint was made, the complaint would not be treated as a time bar under dispute resolution—otherwise known as DISP—complaint rules.

Turn 60 · Speaker unlabelled

Despite concerns being raised, the FOS reaffirmed its position and reopened closed complaints that had been previously rejected as they were out of time. Barclays, NatWest, Vanquis and Santander took the unusual step of taking the FOS’s decision through a judicial review, and won. In fact, the High Court ruling found that the FOS’s interpretation was a fundamental error of law. The court confirmed that the FOS’s jurisdiction is defined and constrained by the FCA’s DISP rules, and that the ordinary meaning of an event is a one-off occurrence rather than the credit relationship as a whole. That is the most recent and clearest example of the FOS extending its jurisdictions. As UK Finance put it, it meant that firms had to go through protracted litigation at a sizeable cost to establish historic lending decisions that fell outside the FOS’s remit.

Turn 61 · Speaker unlabelled

That leads to another example, related to motor finance. In this case, the FOS is not solely at fault, but it found that the discretionary commission arrangements had not been adequately disclosed, stating that that could create an unfair relationship under section 140A of the Consumer Credit Act 1974. The issue is that the FOS took a general approach, stating that firms were acting unfairly, when they were in fact complying with the disclosure rules enforced at the time. The other, more important issue is that its intervention contributed to a retrospective change in the law, making firms retrospectively liable.
