Turn 73 · Florence Eshalomi

The clause closes a specific gap in the residential PEEPs regime. Residential PEEPs support residents whose ability to evacuate without assistance may be compromised by a physical or cognitive impairment or condition. The existing regulations require a person-centred assessment but do not require it to consider relevant fire safety risks and mitigating measures within the resident’s own flat. This clause allows those risks to be considered, with the resident’s consent, as part of the existing process. It also requires the responsible person to identify and support reasonable and proportionate measures that could reduce those risks. The clause will ensure that the assessment reflects the resident’s individual circumstances and provides a more complete picture of the risks they may face in a fire. Turn 74 · Florence Eshalomi

Clause 99 addresses a gap in the current building safety regime relating to section 24 managers, who are appointed by the first-tier tribunal to take over the day-to-day management of a leasehold building where there have been significant failings by the freeholder or managing agent. They often become responsible for repairing and maintaining the common parts. Under the current framework, a section 24 manager may nevertheless lack building safety duties. This can create uncertainty, disagreement or conflicting action over who is responsible for complying with the Building Safety Act and engaging with the Building Safety Regulator. The clause allows a section 24 manager to become the accountable person where the tribunal order gives them responsibility for repairing or maintaining the common parts of a higher-risk building. Turn 76 · Florence Eshalomi

Financial penalties are an important enforcement tool for supporting compliance with the Bill’s provisions. They should incentivise compliance in the first instance, while giving regulators an immediate and effective sanction where serious non-compliance occurs. However, those powers must be underpinned by a fair and robust process. Those facing a penalty should understand why it is being proposed, have an opportunity to make written representations, and be able to challenge the decision on reasonable grounds. That is why clause 102 introduces schedule 3 to provide a common procedure for financial penalties under the Bill, with these important safeguards in place. Penalties must also remain meaningful. Turn 77 · Speaker unlabelled

Paragraph 8 deals with the daily penalties that will continue to build up while the non-compliance continues. Such penalties apply where a responsible entity continues to fail to assess their building or continues to fail to prepare or update its remediation compliance plan. Because the final amounts cannot be known when the penalty is first imposed, paragraph 8 allows the authority to subsequently issue a payment notice. Such a notice will confirm how long the failure continued, how much of the daily penalty has accrued and the amount that must be paid. Before issuing that notice, the authority must be satisfied beyond reasonable doubt that the failure continued throughout the period set out in the notice. Any days for which the recipient demonstrates a reasonable excuse must be excluded from the amount payable.

Turn 80 · Speaker unlabelled

That power can be exercised by giving a notice in writing to the recipient. Paragraph 10 provides that if a person fails to pay all or part of a financial penalty due under a final notice or payment notice, the unpaid amount carries interest. Such interest will be at the rate specified in section 17 of the Judgments Act 1838. Where the authority considers it appropriate, it may require immediate payment of any outstanding penalty and any unpaid interest. The authority may also recover all or part of the unpaid penalty through a county court or High Court.
