Turn 9 · Speaker unlabelled

In the last Session, which ran from the start of this Parliament until April this year, the average turnout for Divisions in your Lordships’ House was just 39%. That was a Session which saw the nationalisation of the railways, the expulsion of the hereditary Peers, an overhaul of our planning laws, sweeping new employment rights and a ban on anyone born after 2009 ever being able to buy a cigarette. On these and sundry other matters, six in 10 of your Lordships chose not to express a view, as is their right—as it is the right of 75% of the sponsors of the noble Baroness’s amendment not to be present for our deliberations in Committee today. Indeed, only twice this century, in the 2013-14 Session and the 2019-21 Session, did the average turnout in Divisions surpass 50%.

Turn 10 · Speaker unlabelled

In the House of Commons, the average turnout was higher, but still only 73% in the last Session, meaning that more than a quarter of MPs did not vote in the average Division. As the caveat on the parliamentary website and in every respectable news outlet is always clear to point out, there can be many reasons for that. People might be genuinely undecided. They might disagree with the binary choice that is being presented. They might think that they can better serve their constituents by performing some duty in their constituency that night, or might choose not to take their seats in Parliament at all as a point of principle. They might be ill, have caring responsibilities or be otherwise indisposed. Politicians, after all, are human too, and the pairing system and some of the advances that have been made in both Houses of Parliament reflect those practical and human considerations.

Turn 12 · Speaker unlabelled

The noble Baroness and noble Lords who have brought this amendment have done so in a considered way. They are not seeking to insert a compulsory voting mechanism into the Bill, but simply to compel the Secretary of State, within a defined period, to bring proposals before us which Parliament can consider. If we do so and decide to compel our fellow citizens to turn out and vote in parliamentary and local elections, we ought to be seen to be practising what we preach. At the moment, on this as on so many other matters, the electorate have a lot that we can learn from.

That is one of the many suggestions that we need to think further on.
