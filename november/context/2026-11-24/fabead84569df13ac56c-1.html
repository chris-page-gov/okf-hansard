Turn 4 · The Deputy Leader of the House of Lords (Lord Collins of Highbury) (Lab)

My Lords, while we are disappointed with the tribunal’s judgment, we must accept it, because the tribunal does not provide a route for appeal for these types of decisions. Successive Governments have adopted “neither confirm nor deny” on the use of investigatory powers notices because confirming whether access is available to specific services could enable terrorists and criminals to evade detection by avoiding those services, making us all less safe.

Turn 5 · Lord Clement-Jones (LD)

My Lords, the tribunal found that the Government’s own briefings to the Times and the Financial Times, authorised at a senior level, undermined the secrecy they were asking the court to protect. Will the Government now commit to publishing the number of technical capability notices in force, and to giving the Investigatory Powers Commissioner a duty to report publicly on how they affect encryption, so that Parliament does not have to rely on litigation by Apple, Privacy International and Liberty to learn how these powers are used?

Turn 6 · Lord Collins of Highbury (Lab)

The judgment applies only in the narrow circumstances of this case. There is no wider implication for “neither confirm nor deny” as a policy. The judgment confirmed only that a TCN had been given to Apple. We are not publishing the full details of the notice and the judgment does not mean that every detail of the notice can be safely disclosed. The matter remains sub judice.

Turn 8 · Lord Collins of Highbury (Lab)

As with all technical capability notices, the notice has been double-locked by the Home Secretary and a judicial commissioner, after a thorough assessment of necessity and proportionality. “Neither confirm nor deny” is not a means to avoid scrutiny; extensive oversight of all investigatory powers is exercised by the Investigatory Powers Commissioner—a Member of this House—with exceptional diligence and regard to sensitivity and to the security of us all.
