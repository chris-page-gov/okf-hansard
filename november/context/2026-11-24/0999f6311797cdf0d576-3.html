Turn 18 · Speaker unlabelled

I freely acknowledge the probing nature of this amendment. As a change on its own, it would produce some very odd circumstances, not least because, with the current ward boundaries, some seats would have more or the same number of representatives as residents. This may remind noble Lords of recent elections in your Lordships’ House for hereditary positions. But then again, the current arrangements are pretty odd. Despite attempts by the corporation to drum up interest with the so-called Speak for the City campaign, in the last elections, 45 of the 100 common councilmen faced no opposition—there was no election. By contrast, in the previous election, there were 19 unopposed appointments. It is heading even further away from a thin veneer of democratic cover.

Turn 19 · Speaker unlabelled

The issues are much bigger than that. The franchise is the issue. Each company that has offices within the square mile of the City can appoint voters based on the number of employees working in those offices. The company appoints voters as they see fit; they are company men and women. The City of London (Ward Elections) Act 2002 contains provisions that I do not believe are checked up on: a qualifying body—an employer—can appoint one voter in five employees and one for every five thereafter, up to 50; for employers of more than 50 employees, there is another voter for each successive 50. The directions that I do not think are checked up on is that those voters should reflect the composition of the workforce, as far as possible. There is a requirement of connection by employment within the City in the past year, or five years in the last 10 years.
