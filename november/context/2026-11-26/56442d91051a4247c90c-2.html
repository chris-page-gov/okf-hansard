Turn 9 · Baroness Bull (CB)

My Lords, my noble friend Lord Tarassenko, who is not in his place, chaired the Maths Horizons project, as noble Lords will know. He gave evidence to the Numeracy for Life Committee strongly supporting the use of AI tutors, particularly for students who were struggling with maths. He told us that they should not give the answers, of course, but should hint and help students develop the problem-solving skills and mathematical reasoning they needed. The committee recommended that the Government should evaluate the use of AI-enabled maths tutoring tools, identifying the most efficacious, as well as the safeguards that are needed. Have the Government taken account of that recommendation and will they act?

Turn 10 · Baroness Ramsey of Wall Heath (Lab)

The DfE is working with teachers, schools and industry to co-create and test AI tutoring tools. The programme is exploring how AI tutoring tools can support teaching and personalised learning in the manner that the noble Baroness has described. It is underpinned by the DfE’s generative AI product safety standards for education, which provide clear expectations for safe AI tool design. Tools meeting robust safety and pedagogy requirements will progress to trials and evaluation through the national EdTech Testbed programme. The evidence from the test bed programme and AI tutoring will help establish whether technology is safe, effective and beneficial, which will support schools to make decisions informed by evidence.

Turn 12 · Baroness Ramsey of Wall Heath (Lab)

I wonder whether that suggests something about the noble Lord’s music tastes. Evidence shows that edtech can support teaching and learning, pupil outcomes, inclusion and teacher workload, but impact varies by technology and context. The DfE’s Technology in Schools survey provides nationally representative evidence on schools’ use of technology and AI, the benefits and the barriers to effective implementation. The Education Endowment Foundation’s evidence base highlights the importance of pedagogy and effective implementation in determining the impact of digital technology. Evidence on newer technologies, particularly AI-enabled personalised learning, remains mixed and developing. We are keeping a close watch on the results of all that research, including on inclusion, as the noble Lord suggests.

Turn 14 · Baroness Ramsey of Wall Heath (Lab)

I was going to make a joke about people who trained when I was a barrister and quill pens, but I will not. As part of our Digital Lives research programme, we have commissioned a systematic review of the impact of digital technologies on children’s cognitive development, with a particular emphasis on generative AI. We have established an educational neuroscience policy research centre as a two-year pilot. The goal is to understand how the brain learns and then use that knowledge to improve teaching methods, learning environments and educational outcomes, just as the noble Baroness is wondering about. One strand will focus on AI-enhanced technologies, education and developmental outcomes.

Turn 16 · Baroness Ramsey of Wall Heath (Lab)

That is indeed concerning data from my noble friend. I understand that this data from Bark is from outside the UK. However, our statutory guidance is clear that schools and colleges should adopt an all-school approach to online safety, including limiting children’s exposure to harmful content online. This includes having appropriate filtering and monitoring systems in place and reviewing their effectiveness at least once every academic year. Our filtering and monitoring standards provide detailed information and guidance to support settings to meet those safeguarding duties.
