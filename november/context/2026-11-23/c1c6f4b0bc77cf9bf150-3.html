Turn 17 · Speaker unlabelled

The Government have said that the costs will be met from existing NHS budgets, so this is not about finding money for innovative treatments. It is about how much of the NHS budget will be redirected towards higher medicine prices—one might say to the profits of big pharma—and what that means for everything else that the health service needs to provide. Research published in the British Medical Journal by Professor Karl Claxton and colleagues estimated that the cumulative additional cost to the NHS in England could reach £44.7 billion over the lifetime of the arrangement. The Government dispute that figure, but the fact that there is such a substantial difference between estimates makes it all the more important that we have proper scrutiny and transparency of the costs.

Turn 18 · Speaker unlabelled

The Nuffield Trust has warned that the arrangement risks seeing more NHS funding diverted towards treatments that deliver lower health benefits per pound spent. There are also questions about the balance of the deal itself. The UK has made long-term commitments to increase medicine spending and change the way medicines are assessed. In return, the US has offered tariff protections that are time limited. The House of Lords International Agreements Committee questioned this imbalance, asking how the Government have assessed the difference between the UK’s domestic commitments and the temporary nature of the US concessions.

Turn 19 · Speaker unlabelled

There is also an issue for the devolved nations. In Scotland, medicines are assessed by the Scottish Medicines Consortium rather than NICE. The SMC is not even mentioned in this arrangement, despite the fact that the financial and policy implications will inevitably matter to the Scottish NHS. We need to understand how these commitments will affect different health services across the UK and how these costs will be distributed.

Turn 20 · Speaker unlabelled

I recognise that the arrangement has already been published and came into effect on 2 April. It is also a non-binding political arrangement, rather than a treaty requiring formal ratification, but that does not make parliamentary scrutiny any less necessary. In fact, it makes the absence of scrutiny even more worrying. This amendment would give Parliament the opportunity to consider the arrangement on the basis of published evidence, including a proper assessment of its potential effects on the health service.
