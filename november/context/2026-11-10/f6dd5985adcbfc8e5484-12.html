Turn 89 · Speaker unlabelled

Amendments 14 and 24 seek to replace the appropriateness test for a judge-only trial with one of reasonableness and fairness. Judges will already be considering whether trials could be reasonably conducted with a jury when determining appropriateness for a “judge alone” trial. The right to a fair trial is protected by Article 6 of the European Convention on Human Rights, to which this Government are committed. Trial by judge alone is in and of itself a fair mode of trial. This is about the burden on the jury, not about the jury’s competence.

Turn 90 · Speaker unlabelled

Amendments 15, 16, and 23 seek to replace the public interest test with an “interests of justice” test. The clause is already clear that judges can choose not to make a “judge alone” order if for any reason a jury trial is more appropriate. This would cover the interests of justice. We are going a step further and seeking to ensure that when judges decide whether to order a “judge alone” trial, wider public interest is always considered. That should not come out of the test, but I note the comments made by the noble Viscount, Lord Hailsham, and my noble friend Lady Chakrabarti and we will certainly discuss those further.

Turn 91 · Speaker unlabelled

The noble Viscount, Lord Hailsham, has also asked for judges to take reasonable steps to reduce the complexity and length of the trial. The Criminal Procedure Rules already require judges to deal with cases “efficiently and expeditiously”. Furthermore, Clause 3, as I have said, already contains robust safeguards. Decisions about judicial deployment and case allocation are matters for the judiciary and there is no need for the additional statutory approval requirement in Amendment 19 for those functions to be exercised appropriately.

Turn 92 · Speaker unlabelled

Amendments 11, 18 and 28 would add a requirement to primary legislation for a judge-only order to be made at a hearing. We already expect judges to order a hearing where necessary, and parties will, rightly, always have the opportunity to make representations, but cases can and do change during proceedings, and that is why it is important for Crown Court judges to have the flexibility to determine how and when to make an order based on the individual nature of a case.

Turn 93 · Speaker unlabelled

Regarding Amendments 20, 21 and 25, creating a separate interlocutory appeal route would risk delay and additional litigation in cases that are already likely to be lengthy and complex—it is inherent in the way that this clause is drafted that that will be the case—and for a mode of trial that is fair under Article 6 of the convention. In all cases, decisions under Clause 3 would be made by a Crown Court judge applying a detailed statutory framework and a number of strict safeguards. As with all other Crown Court trials, the ordinary appeal framework would continue to apply following conviction. That means, in the Government’s view, that Amendment 5 is also nugatory.

Turn 94 · Speaker unlabelled

Finally, Amendments 26 and 27 seek to impose time limits on when reasoned judgments must be delivered. The Bill already requires a reasoned judgment to be given at the time of the verdict or as soon as reasonably practicable afterwards. Judges are required under the Criminal Procedure Rules to deal with cases “efficiently and expeditiously” and to “discourage delay” and we are confident that they will deliver judgments in a timely and responsible manner. We do see that that is important. Requiring the judiciary to produce written judgments in every case would be unduly burdensome and was not recommended by the noble and learned Lord, Lord Leveson. If a judge considers that a written judgment is appropriate, they are able and free to provide one.

Turn 95 · Speaker unlabelled

This clause, as I have set out, has robust safeguards—with eligible offences, excluded offences, a public interest test, an any-other-reason-for-jury trial catch-all, party representations and judicial decision-making. These protections will ensure that jury trial remains the norm but for a few exceptional complex or lengthy cases where the burden on ordinary people is not proportionate. It is for this reason that I hope the noble Viscount, Lord Hailsham, will withdraw his amendment.

Turn 96 · Lord Marks of Henley-on-Thames (LD)

My Lords, before the Minister sits down, and certainly before the noble Viscount, Lord Hailsham, stands up, I would just question the Minister’s assertion that, because there is an appeal against a conviction that would continue, Amendment 25 is otiose or not needed. Under new Section 42A(5) on page 6 of the Bill, there is no right of appeal against a decision to make or not to make an order. If it is intended that a decision to make or not to make an order under this section could be raised as a ground of appeal in the Court of Appeal following conviction, how is that clear in view of the bar on appeals under new subsection (5)?
