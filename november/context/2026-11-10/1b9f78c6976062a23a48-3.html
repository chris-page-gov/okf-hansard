Turn 17 · Lord Sikka (Lab)

My Lords, hiking the interest rate is actually a wrong policy. The current level of inflation is caused by profiteering and supply chain constraints, not by excessive cash sloshing around in households. Some 25.3 million people already live below the minimum income standard. The higher interest rate will force households, businesses and government to hand over wealth to banks, boosting their profits without any additional investment. The right policy should be to curb profiteering. Does the Minister agree? If so, what will he do?

Turn 19 · Lord Clarke of Nottingham (Con)

Will the Government not accept that they face a serious debt crisis, which, admittedly, they inherited? We now have public debt at almost 100% of GDP—more than 90%—and a colossal amount of the tax that the Government collect has to be paid on an ever-mounting level of interest. Will the Government stop saying that they will stick to the fiscal rules, which means that they will stop borrowing in a few years’ time, and take some urgent action to win back the confidence of the market by chasing obvious targets, such as cutting the growth of the welfare budget and scrapping the triple lock to start with?

Turn 20 · Lord Pitt-Watson (Lab)

I agree with many of the noble Lord’s comments. Our debt is way too high and the interest we are paying on the burden of that is very serious. How we go about righting that—as he says, there is a long history, and it is unhelpful to go into it—is a matter of judgment and balance, and it needs to be done over time. I go back to the comments of the chief economist of the IMF: of all the countries it is reviewing, she picks the UK out as the one that, basically, stands out. I hear that there may be some moves a little this way and some a little that way, but I hope that the House will acknowledge the seriousness with which this issue is being treated.

Turn 22 · Lord Pitt-Watson (Lab)

This is an interesting economic argument, but the institutional structure in the UK is that we have an independent Bank of England. That is seen as a strength for the UK in controlling inflation and those other economic effects, which in turn underpins growth for the future. I think that, in general, there is cross-party agreement that that is a good way of doing things.
