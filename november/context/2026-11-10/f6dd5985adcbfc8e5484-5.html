Turn 33 · Speaker unlabelled

To turn specifically to Amendment 2, the reforms proposed do not alter the allocation framework. The courts will continue to determine the appropriate venue by applying the existing statutory test and the independent Sentencing Council’s allocation guideline, which is issued under its statutory functions. The existing guideline already provides a structured and transparent framework based on the seriousness of the offence, the complexity of the case and the likely sentence. Furthermore, our magistrates and district judges are independent office holders who take a judicial oath to act independently, impartially and fairly. Magistrates’ courts are already required to give reasons for their allocation decisions. Nothing in this Bill changes this long-established process. Magistrates’ courts routinely make allocation decisions and have done so for many years. This is bread-and-butter work for magistrates and district judges. These reforms do not transfer allocation decisions to a new decision-maker or create a new test. They leave the allocation decision with the magistrates’ court, applying the same allocation framework as before. There is no reason to suppose that they will not be well able to undertake that task. Turn 36 · Lord Marks of Henley-on-Thames (LD)

I am just concerned to question whether the noble and learned Baroness is right in her argument that allocation decisions regularly made by the magistrates are the end of the matter, because, of course, in cases triable either way, the defendant has the right of election. The magistrates may say, “Well, we take the view that this case can be tried by the magistrates”. The right of election then supersedes that to give the defendant the right to choose jury trial, so it is wrong to say that the final allocation decision is taken by magistrates. I do not know whether that does not make a difference. I suggest that it does make a difference to the question of whether there ought to be a right of appeal.
