Turn 84 · Lord Marks of Henley-on-Thames (LD)

Yes, I share that concern. Actually, it goes further. I do not believe that issues of dishonesty divide neatly into white-collar issues and other issues. Just as juries are told that dishonesty is a matter for them, that they know the ordinary meaning of dishonesty and they recognise it when they see it, I believe that is true of juries at any level. Ultimately, when questions of dishonesty are tried in relation to white-collar crimes that may be complex, ordinary citizens, and a jury of ordinary citizens, can nevertheless tell whether that individual defendant is being dishonest just as well as a judge can. That is certainly so when the wisdom of 12 is combined.

Turn 88 · Speaker unlabelled

Amendments 14, 15, 16, 17, 23 and 24 seek to change the drafting of the test, but none of these amendments would provide materially greater protection than is already provided by Clause 3, as I have set out. Regarding Amendment 17, juries are well equipped to understand complex evidence. I say to both the noble Lord, Lord Marks, and to my noble friend Lady Chakrabarti that it is no part of the Government’s case that juries will find it difficult or impossible to understand the evidence. That is not the case we are making. We entirely agree that it should be appropriately presented by experienced advocates. This policy is not about juror comprehension, or indeed white-collar versus other offences, but about the disproportionate burden that very long fraud and financial offences trials place on jurors. I want to make that distinction crystal-clear.
