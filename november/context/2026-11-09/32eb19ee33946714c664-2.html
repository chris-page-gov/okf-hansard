Turn 10 · Lord Leong (Lab)

My Lords, the United States’ actions have exposed serious structural weaknesses, particularly in dispute settlement, but abandoning the WTO would leave British businesses more vulnerable to arbitrary tariffs and economic pressure. For example, UK trade with non-FTA partners based on most favoured nation terms was something like £605 billion in 2025. So the WTO’s rules still underpin most global trade, and 72% of global trade operated under MFN terms at the end of February. We must engage with the United States in pressing it for reform and work with partners on practical agreements and interim arbitration.

Turn 12 · Lord Leong (Lab)

My Lords, I totally agree with my noble friend. British businesses must be able to compete fairly, and our trade strategy calls for stronger action on subsidies that distort markets and create excess capacity. As major economies, we share the same responsibility as China to support the global trading system. Our bilateral discussions on WTO reform are focused on making the WTO more relevant, flexible and accessible. At senior official level between the UK and China, the WTO working group took place earlier this year in Beijing, covering a level playing field, development and reform issues. Together we have a common agenda to make sure that the WTO functions and works for everybody.

Turn 14 · Lord Leong (Lab)

The noble Lord knows that we have to reform the WTO. As it currently stands, the dispute settlement is not functioning. It has been more or less paralysed since 2019, when the US refused to appoint any judges to its panel. Until such time that we get the dispute settlement resolved, we have the MPIA, which is the system that the noble Lord, Lord Lamont, mentioned. We are still coming together to try to get some resolution to where there is excess subsidy or dumping of cheap goods in this country. We need to resolve this through the system that we currently have.
