Turn 25 · The Lord Bishop of Manchester

My Lords, we have rightly been reflecting on the trauma that victims and survivors face in court and trying to see how we can reduce that. One of the things that make trauma worse is the sheer length of the delay between when offences are committed and initially detected and when trials happen. What work are the Government doing with the Crown Prosecution Service to try to see that serious sexual offenders are brought to justice more quickly, so that survivors and victims do not have to wait so long to give their evidence?

Turn 26 · Lord Lemos (Lab)

I entirely agree with the right reverend Prelate on the damage that delays, particularly in the Crown Court but also in the magistrates’ courts, do to victims in those circumstances. That is why the Government are legislating in the Courts and Tribunals Bill. It is not a question simply of working with the Crown Prosecution Service, which of course we will, and already, do; it is with the specific intention of reducing waiting times and the damage those do to victims that we are legislating.

Turn 28 · Lord Lemos (Lab)

My noble friend is clearly right that if the experience of being a victim and giving evidence in these cases is so traumatising or retraumatising, one can understand—particularly, as the right reverend Prelate said, in the context of long delays—why victims would find that a really serious problem. As I have said, that leads to us not having the track record that we would wish for in prosecuting these cases. The specific initiative that my noble friend refers to is a civil society initiative, and I welcome civil society initiatives such as that as part of an ongoing social campaign to change this question of rape.
