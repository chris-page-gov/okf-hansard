Turn 9 · Speaker unlabelled

Fourthly—this is at the heart of it—there should be one power of intervention, and a serious one. Where a board is seriously and persistently failing, with a real risk to safety, quality or public money, or in an emergency, the Secretary of State may appoint commissioners to run its functions. That is the model we use for failing councils, so it is not reinventing any wheel. It comes with safeguards: notice and reasons; outside an emergency, a right to make representations and consultation with local government; a time limit; regular review; every order and its reason being laid before Parliament; and, outside that route, no general power to direct ICBs on how they do their job.

Turn 10 · Speaker unlabelled

The merits are simple. It is clear about what the Secretary of State wants to achieve, and it tells Parliament. It draws a clean line between outcomes and operations and it gives ICBs the room that the 10-year plan says they should have as strategic commissioners. It keeps a strong power to intervene but saves it for serious failures and emergencies. It is accountable to Parliament, as the national outcomes, the progress report and every intervention order are laid before it. A direction published after the event, with no procedure at all, is simply not acceptable.

Turn 13 · Speaker unlabelled

Could the Minister give the Committee examples of this? Will she also publish illustrative directions before Report? Could the Government not achieve a framework through national outcomes, Clause 16 regulations and NICE guidance, rather than a general power of direction? Why are directions published, rather than laid? Why is there no procedure at all, when the department itself calls such instruments “subordinate legislation”? The really important question she needs to answer is this: how does any of this square with the promise of devolution and earned autonomy, when the centre ends up with more power over ICBs than NHS England ever had?
