Turn 65 · Speaker unlabelled

The amendment seeks to restore public provision as the starting point. It recognises that there may be situations—particularly after the past couple of decades—where a private provider is necessary because there is no reasonable public sector alternative available within a clinically appropriate timeframe. But that should be the exception, not an alternative that is routinely considered alongside public provision, as though the two were interchangeable. There is a fundamental difference between bringing in private capacity to meet an urgent and unavoidable need and designing a health service around the assumption that private providers should have a permanent role in delivering it.

Turn 66 · Speaker unlabelled

We must not forget that private companies do not provide NHS services as an act of charity; they operate within commercial structures, with shareholders, management costs and an expectation of profit. Public money that flows into those structures is money no longer available to support public provision. We all know what has happened where private providers, for example, take those patients who need a simple hip replacement but leave those patients with multiple comorbidities and complicating factors to the NHS. Even if that apparently simple hip placement goes wrong, it is the NHS that has to pick up the pieces. The public sector remains the provider of last resort, but it does not always retain the infrastructure, staff or capacity needed to fulfil that responsibility.

Turn 69 · Speaker unlabelled

Amendment 51, the final part of this package, proposes a duty to provide NHS services publicly. There is a question that this Bill, for all its wide-ranging impacts, and the Government have not really addressed: what do we want the NHS to look like in 20 or 30 years’ time? Do we want a health service that has the people, buildings, expertise and resources to provide care directly as a public service accountable to the communities it serves? Or do we want a service that increasingly acts as a commissioner, purchasing healthcare from a collection of private companies, with the public sector left to manage the contracts, fill in the gaps and pick up the pieces when things go wrong, as they went so wrong in Hinchingbrooke?

Turn 71 · Speaker unlabelled

Research by the BMA has estimated that spending on non-NHS providers, including direct and indirect commissioning, increased by 70% in real terms between 2012-13 and 2020-21, rising from £10.8 billion to £18.3 billion. With the current provision in the Bill, the Government appear to want to lock that in place. The BMA has warned about the consequences for NHS training and workforce development when independent providers take on increasing volumes of elective procedures, and we all know and frequently discuss the issues around the level of workforce training and workforce retention that the NHS has. If NHS hospitals lose the opportunity to carry out routine procedures, they also lose the opportunity to train staff, develop clinical expertise and maintain the capacity needed to provide more complex care.
