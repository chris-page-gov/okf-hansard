Turn 197 · Speaker unlabelled

Access and continuity are not mutually exclusive. Timely access matters, but patients who see their own GPs consult less frequently, with a recent study finding that the average interval between appointments was around 9% longer. Continuity therefore improves access for everyone. Barriers exist: only 45% of GPs report having enough time to provide continuity, according to the Royal College of GPs. That brings me back to the amendment. It would establish a simple but important principle by simply requiring the Secretary of State to promote continuity as an essential part of NHS services in and outside hospitals.

Turn 198 · Speaker unlabelled

I therefore ask the Minister three questions. First, how will the Government turn their commitment to restoring the family doctor into a genuine continuity of care so that patients can see the same GP rather than simply a named GP on their records? Secondly, how will they ensure that the neighbourhood health service strengthens rather than fragments the relationship between patients and their regular clinicians? Finally, if the Government are not minded to accept the amendment, what mechanism will ensure that continuity is given proper weight in decisions about the design and commissioning of NHS services?
