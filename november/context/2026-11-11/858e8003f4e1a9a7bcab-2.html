Turn 11 · Speaker unlabelled

Alan Milburn’s interim review of young people not in education, employment or training describes the damaging cycle: poor mental health can push young people out of education, training and employment, but exclusion can then deepen their difficulties by undermining their confidence, increasing their isolation and making it even harder for them to find a way back. We know that prolonged unemployment can be damaging to mental and physical health, but we also know that meaningful employment, with the right support, can help people rebuild their confidence, develop relationships and rediscover a sense of purpose. Surely these are the foundations on which both the prevention of and recovery from mental illness can be built.

Turn 12 · Speaker unlabelled

I emphasise again that not all work is good work. Insecure employment, unreasonable demands, poor relationships and a lack of support can damage mental health rather than improve it. Zero-hours contracts and unpredictable working patterns may increase anxiety rather than provide stability. Good work is work that is safe, fairly paid and suited to the person and that offers supportive relationships and reasonable adjustments where needed.

Turn 13 · Speaker unlabelled

For a young person struggling with anxiety, this might mean flexible hours, a trusted colleague or buddy and time to find their feet. For someone recovering from serious mental illness, it might mean supported employment alongside continuing clinical care, as explained so well by the noble Lord, Lord Singh. As he has argued, employment support must be integrated with appropriate healthcare rather than treated as an entirely separate responsibility. We must not force people into work before they are ready, but nor should we allow a lack of imagination or appropriate support to exclude people who have something to contribute.

Turn 14 · Speaker unlabelled

I draw attention to something that is sometimes overlooked in our discussions of employment and mental health: the importance of belonging. Belonging means being known as a person. It means having a voice, being welcomed and being missed when we are absent. A good workplace can offer this, but so can a book club, a theatre group, a volunteering opportunity or a community project. Through my involvement with Books Beyond Words, a charity that I founded, I have seen just how important sharing stories and participating in a group can be. They can help people who have been isolated to develop friendships, express themselves and discover new confidence.
