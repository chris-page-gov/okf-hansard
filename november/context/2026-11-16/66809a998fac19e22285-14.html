Turn 105 · Lord Macdonald of River Glaven (CB)

My Lords, I do not think there is any doubt at all that jury equity exists; the Court of Appeal has said so on more than one occasion. But does the noble Lord really foresee no practical or philosophical difficulties for a jury directed by a judge that, if they are sure beyond reasonable doubt—or sure as the phrase is now put—that a defendant is guilty, they should acquit him, when defence counsel says, “Even if you are sure on the evidence that he’s guilty, you should acquit him”? Does the noble Lord see no difficulty in that as a proposition at the end of a trial? Can he not conceive of it causing some difficulty in the jury room?

Turn 106 · Lord Marks of Henley-on-Thames (LD)

Not if the position is correctly put. The noble Lord, Lord Macdonald, just said that the judge should direct that they will convict if they are satisfied so that they are sure. That is not the law of the land. The law of the land is that they may not convict unless they are sure. But they are still entitled, because of the principle of jury equity, to take the view, “There is evidence that could persuade us to convict, but we are not going to do it”. I do not see why the law of the land should be interpreted in such a way that the jury is disentitled from hearing, from the judge as well as from counsel, that the principle of jury equity entitles them to say, “We are going to acquit in conscience”, just as Clive Ponting and others were acquitted because of the conscience of the jury, which trumps the strict law. That is the principle. I am happy to give way.

Turn 107 · Lord Macdonald of River Glaven (CB)

The noble Lord invites me to come back, so I will. In that case, every direction given by a judge on the burden of proof and the standard and quality of the evidence would have to be accompanied by the further direction, “Never mind any of that; if your conscience tells you to acquit, you can acquit”. That is a preposterous position to adopt.
