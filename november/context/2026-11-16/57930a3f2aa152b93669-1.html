Turn 5 · Baroness Gill (Lab)

My Lords, I thank my noble friend for that. But last month a Demos report revealed that the UK is a total outlier in Europe for allowing politicians to host news and current affairs programmes. Out of 37 countries surveyed, 35 had absolutely no recent examples of this. The UK stands virtually alone, yet Ofcom’s enforcement remains incredibly lax, exploiting a thin distinction between news and current affairs to avoid taking action. Given that the most advanced democracies strictly ban these practices, what actions does my noble friend the Minister plan to take to close these loopholes and protect the credibility of our democracy?

Turn 6 · Baroness Mackenzie of Sherwood (Lab)

I thank my noble friend. As she said, the Demos report sets out that approaches taken on this issue vary across our European neighbours. The Government remain concerned about politicians presenting programmes that could reasonably be interpreted as news or current affairs. There is a clear rationale to strengthen the rules in the UK, and we are considering options to tackle this problem. We will set out our decision as soon as we are able to.

Turn 7 · Baroness Bonham-Carter of Yarnbury (LD)

My Lords, surveys have repeatedly shown that broadcast news is by far the most trusted news source in the UK. Turn 8 · Baroness Mackenzie of Sherwood (Lab)

As the noble Baroness says, the distinction was not clearly made in the 2003 Communications Act, which, in my former life as a special adviser, I played a role in—for which, apologies. The world has changed since then, and that is why our media Green Paper set out a pathway for us to work on this issue. I know that the noble Lord, Lord Hall, raised a series of very sound points, which he is very well qualified to make from his former life, about rethinking the distinctions between news and current affairs. I hope that we may be able to report further actions and thoughts on that as soon as possible.
