Turn 81 · Baroness Goudie (Lab)

My Lords, Amendment 62 is in my name and those of my noble friend Lady Chakrabarti and the noble Viscount, Lord Hailsham. I thank them both. At Second Reading, I ended my speech on defendants who were not brought to court on time. I raised the human rights obligation that we have to prisoners awaiting trial and on remand. When delays cause trials to collapse, defendants face longer waits for decisions. Their families do not know what is happening. That determines their liberty, and victims face prolonged trauma.

Turn 83 · Speaker unlabelled

The amendment would do four things. First, the Lord Chancellor must publish a monthly statement on the late delivery of defendants to criminal hearings. Secondly, the statement must name the prisons and the contractors involved. Thirdly, it would mean that, when a hearing was delayed by late arrival, the court recorded it and any comment from the judge or the magistrate and sent it to the Lord Chancellor and the Lady Chief Justice. Fourthly, if one provider’s delays exceeded 1% of its journeys for three months running, the Lord Chancellor must review that contract.

Turn 84 · Speaker unlabelled

The theme running through the Bill is that delays matter. Behind every late van is a hearing that does not start: victims and witnesses wait in the corridors; jurors sit idle; counsel, interpreters and court staff wait too long; and the defendant on remand, who is presumed innocent, waits another day for a decision on their liberty. Between February and June this year, barristers sent to the Bar Council 213 reports of late delivery—about 13 a week—from 69 courts and 59 prisons across all six circuits. One-quarter of those delays lasted more than five hours. That is quite unacceptable.

Turn 85 · Speaker unlabelled

The Government’s figures look very different. In April 2026, Ministers told us that escort contractors delivered prisoners on time in 99.91% of cases. In some cases, delay may be due to the contractors. In others, as the noble and learned Lord, Lord Leveson, noted at Second Reading, a van may be late because a remand prisoner has not been housed in a prison near the court. That is exactly why the amendment names prisons as well as contractors.

Turn 86 · Speaker unlabelled

The amendment is not about blaming one company; it is about seeing the journey from the cell to the dock. The key figure that we wish to understand is whether the defendant was in the dock when the judge sat. For the victim, the jury and the defendant, it makes no difference whose fault it is; the court time is lost either way.

Turn 87 · Speaker unlabelled

I understand that courts already file court exception reports when a hearing is delayed, and Ministers have told Parliament that they cover prisons, courts and prisoner issues, as well as the escort contractor. Those reports go to the Prison and Probation Service. What is missing is publication. I welcome the steps that the Government have taken, including the ministerial chaired Prisoner Delivery Oversight Board and the work with Transport for London. Publication would strengthen that work. It would let this House see whether it is succeeding and would allow us to scrutinise the performance of prisoner escort and custody services.

Turn 88 · Speaker unlabelled

I cannot claim that 1% over three months is the perfect trigger, but I would welcome the Minister’s view of the right measures. The principle is simple: persistent failure should prompt a review that Parliament can see. If we are serious about tracking the backlog in criminal courts then we must address the root causes of the delays. This should come before the House as we consider proposals to restrict fundamental safeguards in our criminal courts.
