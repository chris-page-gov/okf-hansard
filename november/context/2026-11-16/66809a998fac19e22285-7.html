Turn 50 · Speaker unlabelled

Our publication about the magistrates’ courts this month set out that we still expect triable “either way” not-guilty trial cases to be dealt with considerably quicker in the magistrates’ courts after our reforms. We estimate that, on average, these cases will be heard in eight to nine months, whereas defendants on bail are currently waiting 18 months on average for this type of case to be heard in the Crown Court. Last time, the noble and learned Lord, Lord Keen of Elie, challenged me on what evidence we had. I am sorry that he is not in his place to hear my answer.

Turn 51 · Speaker unlabelled

As noble Lords discussed last week, as well as speeding up justice for victims, witnesses and defendants, these reforms to the trials of “either way” offences will also have a beneficial impact on the backlog in the Crown Court. Without making these changes, the Crown Court backlog will continue to rise. As a result of these changes and the various other measures the Government have announced, our modelling shows that the Crown Court backlog will peak in 2028 and start to fall in 2028-29, putting the whole system on a more sustainable trajectory, with considerable benefits for victims, witnesses and defendants.

Turn 53 · Speaker unlabelled

The Government are committed to rebuilding the magistracy to 21,000 magistrates by 2028-29. This target is ambitious, but I am pleased to confirm that we are ahead of our stated ambitions, with almost 1,500 appointments made since April—twice as many as during the equivalent period last year. We recognise that magistrates are an important part of a wider workforce in the courts. Some 50 deputy district judges were recruited in 2025, and we identified a reserve list of 86 additional candidates, whose appointments are now under way. Alongside this, there is ongoing recruitment for 30 district judges. Since April 2025, 166 additional legal advisers have been appointed, with a further campaign for 80 legal advisers underway.

Turn 55 · Speaker unlabelled

We are already seeing a substantial increase in the diversity of magistrates, as people from all walks of life sign up to help deliver justice in their local community. I have seen some of the responses, and I have a great deal of respect for the people applying and the way in which they present their commitment to public service as lay people. In 2025-26, 17% of new appointments were from ethnic minority backgrounds, compared with 14% of serving magistrates, and more than a third of new recruits were under 50, compared with less than a fifth of the existing magistracy. Women account for 55% of new appointments, compared with 57% of magistrates currently in post.

Turn 56 · Speaker unlabelled

On the crucial question of the magistrates’ courts’ ability to handle more serious cases, magistrates are already experienced in hearing serious and complex cases in both criminal and family jurisdictions, for example, with complex domestic abuse issues. Youth courts—such as the court in which the noble Baroness, Lady Sater, sits—routinely hear cases involving violent offending and have sentencing powers of up to 24 months. As the noble Baroness, Lady Hamwee, noted, the Judicial College has already begun planning training for magistrates, legal advisers and district judges for the Courts and Tribunals Bill reforms, and the Government have increased funding for training in line with estimates provided by the Judicial College for the additional resources required.
