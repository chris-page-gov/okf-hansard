Furthermore, there is the point that if these reasons are to be considered by the Crown Court on an application for permission to appeal, it means that the magistrates will have to appeal-proof their decisions. That places a greater burden on magistrates to craft comprehensive reasons to show that they have correctly applied the law to the facts, which will pose a greater burden on magistrates, and particularly on lay magistrates. Turn 11 · Speaker unlabelled

In the Government’s response to the Justice Select Committee’s report they rely on the existing case law that says that magistrates have to give only relatively brief reasons for their verdict. That is right, of course, but that is because under the existing law there is an automatic right of appeal on the merits. Thus, succinct reasons, which would otherwise be inadequate, are less problematic. Detailed reasons are not necessary under the current scheme because a convicted defendant can either exercise that automatic right of appeal by way of rehearing or ask the magistrates to provide detailed reasons to form a statement of case to the High Court. All that changes if the magistrates’ reasons become determinative of a Crown Court appeal. Under the new system, for the first time, magistrates’ reasons given at trial will be subject to scrutiny on appeal to the Crown Court.

Turn 12 · Speaker unlabelled

Moreover, my seventh point is that this new system of appeals impacts significantly on access to justice. An issue raised in the discussion of the Bill generally is that legal aid is less easily available in the magistrates’ court than in the Crown Court. The income cut-off is just £22,000, so roughly half the defendants before the magistrates’ court defend themselves, whereas that is very rare in the Crown Court. Introducing a highly legalistic system of appeals will impact on access to justice for many middle-income defendants. Can they realistically be expected to analyse the magistrates’ reasons themselves and identify grounds of appeal?

Turn 15 · Speaker unlabelled

No statistics have been provided as to how many appeals this affects. I suggest that this is not the valid argument that the Minister suggested. Most cases in the magistrates’ court, of their nature because they are summary offences, are more minor than those heard in the Crown Court. The current system, which requires witnesses to give evidence a second time, is a factor that those advising defendants on an appeal would bear in mind. If a defendant drags a victim to the Crown Court to be cross-examined a second time, no doubt the court hearing the appeal would consider that, should it determine that the appeal was unnecessary, in its question of resentencing. That is all factored into the current system, so I am afraid it is not the slam dunk answer that the Minister suggested it might be.
