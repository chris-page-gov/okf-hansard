Turn 124 · Speaker unlabelled

That leaves us with an important question. If, after two decades of judicial guidance, encouragement from the Court of Appeal and repeated recognition of the value of these directions, they nevertheless remain discretionary, are we not entitled to ask whether the existing mechanisms are capable of delivering the consistency that victims deserve? Those judges who already follow best practice will be virtually unaffected by this amendment. Indeed, I suspect that those judges constitute the overwhelming majority, but this amendment is not directed at the majority; it is directed at those cases in which, for whatever reason, best practice is not being followed.

Turn 125 · Speaker unlabelled

I put a simple question to those who oppose this amendment. If every judge already gives these directions whenever they are appropriate, what possible objection can there be to victims knowing that this safeguard is guaranteed by law? If there are cases in which such directions are not being given when they should be—and the available evidence suggests that there are—why should Parliament not act? That, it seems to me, is the real issue for this Committee. This amendment is not about increasing conviction rates; it is about increasing confidence. A defendant who is convicted may appeal, but a victim who watches harmful myths or stereotypes shape a trial and sees the judge do nothing has no equivalent remedy.

Turn 126 · Speaker unlabelled

Too many victims already approach the criminal justice system with profound anxiety. Too many fear that they will be disbelieved. Too many fear that they themselves will become the subject of scrutiny. Too many emerge from the process saying that they wish they had never reported the offence in the first place. Whether every such perception is justified is, in one sense, beside the point. A justice system depends on public confidence. People must believe that they will be treated fairly if they come forward. That confidence is particularly important for these serious offences, which remain significantly underreported.

Turn 128 · Speaker unlabelled

This amendment would not place England and Wales in uncharted territory. We have the advantage of having my noble and learned friend the Advocate-General for Scotland here. Scotland has introduced statutory provisions addressing misconceptions in sexual offence cases. Australian jurisdictions, including Victoria and New South Wales, have adopted legislative approaches requiring judicial intervention where harmful assumptions may affect jury reasoning. The principle therefore has recognised support in comparable jurisdictions without, as far as I am aware, causing any constitutional crisis.
