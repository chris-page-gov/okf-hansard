Turn 17 · Speaker unlabelled

We have had summary justice in England and Wales for a very long time. Summary justice frequently is conducted by lay members of the public who have more limited sentencing powers. The flip side of the coin of summary justice—for which you can occasionally read “rough justice”—is that you have the right of an appeal to the Crown Court. If we take away that right, the consent to the system of summary justice begins to erode, as does confidence in the criminal justice system itself.

Turn 20 · Speaker unlabelled

In an attempt to demonstrate the need for new administration to be put in place for dealing with these applications before the provisions come into effect, I will spend just a moment or two explaining how appeals from the Crown Court currently work when they go to the Court of Appeal Criminal Division. Those appeals are administered by the Criminal Appeal Office under the direction of the registrar, who is a senior lawyer. The office is staffed with lawyers and caseworkers who gather together the papers and organise both the permission stage and the hearing stage, if there is one. The office receives the application, which must set out the grounds of appeal and why the conviction is arguably unsafe or the sentence arguably manifestly excessive. The office then determines what papers the judge, who will in due course look at the permission application, needs to determine that application.

Turn 21 · Speaker unlabelled

Papers on conviction invariably include the summing up, any documents associated with it and, importantly, any parts of the transcript relevant to the grounds of appeal, but only those parts. That decision and determination is made in the Criminal Appeal Office, and there is sometimes argument about it with the advocates supporting an appellant. It is absolutely critical that that happens to avoid the wasteful provision of unnecessary paper. It is the Criminal Appeal Office that makes arrangements for the transcripts to be provided from the recordings made in court. The papers are sent to the judge, and then the Criminal Appeal Office keeps tabs on turnaround to ensure that the papers are dealt with quickly. As a High Court judge who dealt with many such applications, I can attest to that.

Turn 22 · Speaker unlabelled

The Criminal Appeal Office expedites the case or refers it directly for an oral hearing if it is a matter of real urgency—of course, if the new appeal mechanisms involve appeals against sentence from the magistrates’ court, it is quite likely that a number of those appeals will be against immediate short terms of imprisonment. If permission is granted, the office assembles the papers, enabling the court in due course to hear the appeal. Just as importantly, as the office is staffed by lawyers expert in the detail of criminal law, they highlight for the benefit of the judges and advocates points of law that may not have been picked up by the appellant or those advising him or her. Then, as we know, if permission is refused on the papers, there is a right to renew orally in court.
