Turn 9 · Sureena Brackenridge (Wolverhampton North East) (Lab)

I am very grateful to my hon. Friend for securing this important debate. A mother recently came to see me with concerns about her adult daughter’s involvement with a religious organisation locally. She described how her daughter was once very close to the family, but became increasingly isolated. Concerns were also raised about financial contributions she made while she was a student and struggling to pay everyday basic costs. Of course, freedom of religion is a fundamental right, but does my hon. Friend agree that there may be a gap in the support available to families when concerns arise about undue influence, social isolation or financial pressure in organisations that fall outside the scope of existing legislation on coercive and controlling behaviour?

Turn 10 · Sam Carling

My hon. Friend’s intervention lets me make a really important point. Stopping psychological subjection in high-control religious groups is not anti-freedom of religion; it is pro-freedom of religion. People who are trapped do not have free choice, and creating true free choice means making sure that people are free to change their religion or leave a religion. I will shortly come to her point about support.

Turn 12 · Sam Carling

I agree entirely, and it is a very deep perversion of what religion is meant to be. I speak as someone who is not religious but who recognises how much value religion and faith can add to people’s lives. To distort and twist that in such a way is deeply wrong. When people in these cults and high-control groups take the extraordinarily brave step of leaving, despite the immense barriers to doing so both physically and psychologically, who is there to support them and what help can they find? The answer is very little. There is a deep lack of awareness among therapists and care professionals about this deep-reaching type of abuse and harm.

Turn 14 · Sam Carling

I am grateful to my hon. Friend, and I will shortly sing those volunteers praises, because it is very much right that we thank them. People just cannot understand why victims in these groups do not leave early, or why they stayed. The themes of thought control, financial exploitation, isolation from family and friends, and threats against leaving should sound familiar, because they are exactly the things that characterise domestic abuse and coercive relationships. Indeed, the Government’s statutory guidance on what could constitute criminal coercive behaviour puts it as “a range of acts designed to make a person subordinate and/or dependent by isolating them from sources of support, exploiting their resources and capacities for personal gain, depriving them of the means needed for independence, resistance and escape and regulating their everyday behaviour.” That is a very good definition …
