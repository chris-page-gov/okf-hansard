Turn 129 · Speaker unlabelled

Ultimately, the question seems to me to be a straightforward one. If we accept that myths and stereotypes can distort decision-making, if we accept that judicial directions can help address that risk, and if we accept that those directions are not always given consistently, then Parliament is entitled to consider whether a statutory safeguard is required. Best practice is valuable, the Crown Court Compendium is valuable and judicial training is valuable—none of those things is in dispute. However, the question is this: are they sufficient? Where fairness, consistency and public confidence are at stake, best practice alone is not always enough, and sometimes Parliament must act to ensure that best practice becomes universal practice. I beg to move.

Turn 132 · Speaker unlabelled

I feel somewhat intimidated in this company for suggesting that some judges may require more training. I am not a lawyer, but I have seen many times as an advocate for victims that the family courts can make the threats of perpetrators come true. We owe it to children to ensure that the family courts never unwittingly enable and promote coercive and controlling abuse. Only thorough training for everyone involved can achieve this. My amendments seek to ensure that the responsibility is placed upon government to make this training available, resource it fully and monitor its delivery.

Turn 133 · Speaker unlabelled

We all know that it is all too easy to throw the word “training” around as though it were a panacea. I am aware, even in the short time I have been in this House, that we see report after report call for training. Of course it is not a magic bullet, but there is a genuine gap here in knowledge and understanding—a gap that is then filled with myths that run deep in our culture about violence against women. There are spurious but extremely convincing claims by perpetrators and others. My Amendments 53G and 53H seek to close that gap, ensure that it is closed and monitor the difference this makes.
