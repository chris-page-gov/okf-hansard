Turn 142 · Matt Vickers

Together, these four clauses would mean that people who come here illegally are removed, cannot block that removal with last-minute claims, can never come back lawfully, and can never become British citizens. Take a man who lands on a beach in Kent on 1 December 2026, having paid a smuggling gang with no visa and no permission, and having come from France, a safe country. Under new clause 42, he meets all three conditions. He needed leave and did not have it; he entered without permission; and he arrived after 30 June 2026, the day this Bill was introduced. The Home Secretary had to remove him to his own country or a safe third country. He claimed asylum; under new clause 43, that claim would be inadmissible, there would be no appeal and a modern slavery claim or judicial review would not stop the duty either. Turn 143 · Speaker unlabelled

Labour Members will recognise these provisions, because they voted to scrap them. The Illegal Migration Act 2023 placed a duty on the Home Secretary to remove people who came here illegally; it also made their asylum claims inadmissible and barred them from ever getting leave or citizenship. The citizenship ban was enforced from the day that that Act received Royal Assent. In the Border Security, Asylum and Immigration Act 2025, this Government repealed that duty to remove, the inadmissibility provision, the Safety of Rwanda (Asylum and Immigration) Act 2024 and the ban on illegal entrants becoming British citizens.

Turn 144 · Speaker unlabelled

I sat on the Public Bill Committee for the Border Security, Asylum and Immigration Act. We warned that some of its changes would weaken rather than strengthen our ability to deal with illegal migration. Where are those people who arrived illegally and were due to go to Rwanda now? New clauses 42, 43, 55 and 56 would put back what the Government took away. More than 200,000 people have crossed the channel in small boats since 2018. Last year alone, there were 41,472 crossings. They are down this year, and I welcome that, but remember the trajectory: under the previous Conservative Government, crossings fell by a third in 2023 from 45,774 to under 30,000; after the election, they went back up to last year’s 41,472. A fall this year from that higher level is welcome, but it is a recovery, not a record.
