Turn 233 · Speaker unlabelled

As drafted, new clause 46 would apply to all applications and claims made to the Home Office, not simply protection claims, and it could therefore have far-reaching unintended consequences across the wider immigration system. For example, a person who initially came to the United Kingdom as a student and later became eligible to apply as a skilled worker could find a subsequent application treated as inadmissible because it had not been made at the same time as their original application, which I do not believe is the hon. Member’s intention.

Turn 234 · Speaker unlabelled

New clause 46 is also likely be counterproductive. Rather than reducing litigation, excluding claims in that way would risk increasing judicial reviews, as courts would instead be asked to consider whether decision makers had applied the provision correctly in individual cases. The measure is also not clear on what a “material change in circumstances” means in the subsequent claims. As I am sure the Committee will agree, we must grant protection to those who need it, and refuse those who do not, but not all asylum claims are based on objective country conditions. Indeed, many are based on the existence of immutable and inherent characteristics, such as sexuality or belief. There may be circumstances or individual factors that explain why information was not disclosed at an earlier stage. It is right that those factors are considered in the round so that we get to the right decision.

Turn 237 · Speaker unlabelled

The Government are already taking forward fundamental reform of the immigration and asylum appeals system. The new Independent Immigration Appeals Authority, which we have discussed at length, will deliver a single route for appeal, which must be lodged as early as possible. That will remove the scope for bringing multiple appeals, often vexatious and at the last minute before removal, as some claimants do. Late claims will be heard under an expedited process, ensuring that they cannot be used to frustrate a removal. There will be a right for further appeal to a judge-led tribunal only on a point of law. For those reasons, while I recognise the intention behind the provision, and sympathise with the frustrations of the hon. Member for Stockton West, the Government cannot support it as drafted, and I ask that it not be pressed to Division.

Turn 238 · Speaker unlabelled

I also thank the hon. Member for tabling new clause 54, which would prevent any challenge being brought by way of judicial review in relation to decisions made by the Home Office on any protection or immigration claim. It would leave many people with no judicial redress, even when there was an error made in reaching a decision. The Home Office has a power to certify protection and human rights claims where they are clearly unfounded, under section 94 of the Nationality, Immigration and Asylum Act 2002. Certification under section 94 entirely removes the right of appeal.

Turn 239 · Speaker unlabelled

To ensure that there is judicial oversight of refugee claims, and other claims in respect of fundamental rights, certification decisions can be challenged by judicial review. Removing the ability of the courts to review certification decisions would mean that where the Home Office had declared an asylum or human rights claim clearly unfounded, there would be no judicial check on that decision. That risks not only breaching people’s human rights but the UK being in breach of our obligations under the refugee convention, which requires an avenue of independent redress. Breaching the convention is not just about our international obligations; it risks people being sent back to face persecution, torture and a risk to life.

Turn 240 · Speaker unlabelled

So wide ranging is new clause 54 in preventing any court or tribunal from setting aside a decision that it is not clear there would be any rights of appeal at all. The new clause conflicts with the legislation maintaining the right of appeal for immigration and asylum decisions made by the Home Secretary—not to mention that it conflicts with part 1 of the Bill, which establishes the IIAA to hear exactly those appeals. If passed, that is something the courts would no doubt need to determine, leaving scope for confusion and ambiguity in the meantime. That could undermine and delay the setting up of the IIAA while the legal effect of the clause is litigated, as well as risking confusion in the existing immigration and asylum chamber.
