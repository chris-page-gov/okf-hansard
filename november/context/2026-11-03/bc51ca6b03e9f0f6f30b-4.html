Turn 26 · Speaker unlabelled

The real test is not whether a particular Jewish patient can prove that they have received inferior treatment, because by that stage the damage has been done. The real question is whether a reasonable Jewish patient who discovers that their doctor has publicly expressed antisemitic views could still have confidence that they will receive treatment that is fair, impartial and free from prejudice. If the answer to that question is no, continued registration becomes incompatible with fitness to practise as a doctor.

Turn 28 · Speaker unlabelled

The current regulatory framework allows a wide range of sanctions. My contention is that antisemitism should be treated like any other serious form of racism in a profession built entirely on trust. Healthcare professionals enjoy the same political freedoms as everyone else, and rightly so, but professional registration is not an unconditional right; it is a privilege. Society grants that privilege subject to certain standards. Just as we would not tolerate any doctor expressing racist hatred towards Black patients, or anti-Muslim hatred towards Muslim patients, we should not tolerate antisemitic hatred towards Jewish patients.

Turn 30 · Speaker unlabelled

This amendment would protect not only Jewish patients but the reputation of doctors themselves. It seeks to establish a clear and consistent principle: if a healthcare professional is found to have engaged in antisemitic behaviour or speech, as defined in my amendment and by reference to the Government’s own definition, removal from the register should follow. The NHS cannot function without trust. Antisemitism destroys that trust. I commend my amendment to the Committee.
