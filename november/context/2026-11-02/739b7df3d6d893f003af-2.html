Turn 9 · Lord Murphy of Torfaen (Lab)

My Lords, the noble Lord made a very interesting speech, most of which I disagreed with. I understand the issues, but to say that there is no mandate, constitutional basis or any political support for this measure is absolute nonsense. It was in the manifesto of the Labour Party. When people voted in Northern Ireland, Wales, Scotland and England, they voted, among other things, to agree to voting at 16, so I see no conceivable argument against it constitutionally because it is a constitutional principle—the bedrock of our constitution—that a manifesto commitment is the same as a referendum.

Turn 11 · Speaker unlabelled

As the noble Lord said, Wales and Scotland have had these measures for some time and the world has not collapsed, nor have the constitutional heavens come in. In fact, there has been an enormous increase in interest in politics as a result. However, I agree with the noble Lord very much on one point: this has to be accompanied by a really serious improvement in civic education among young people. We do not do it well enough. I spent 17 years of my life teaching youngsters at a small college what used to be called “British constitution”. I hope I did my little bit to improve their knowledge of how they are governed. Turn 12 · Speaker unlabelled

It is also important to point out that there are variations in voting in the United Kingdom, and there is nothing wrong with that. For decades, Northern Ireland had a different system of voting for its regional and provincial governments and for its local authorities than for its parliamentary elections. For the last 26 years, the devolved Administrations in Scotland and Wales have decided that they were able to decide the methods by which people could vote and the constitutional arrangements for their own Parliaments. Again, that has worked extremely well. To undermine that, incidentally, is to undermine the devolution principle on with the Senedd in Wales and the Parliament in Scotland have been elected.
