Turn 166 · Speaker unlabelled

One example of organ donation that noble Lords will be familiar with is that of Max Johnson, who was nine years old when he received a life-saving heart transplant after eight months on the waiting list. His father has spoken of how agonising the wait was. Parents look to the doctors for reassurance and, with organ donation, the wait is the one thing on which they cannot give a measure of reassurance. Max’s heart came from Keira Ball, a nine year-old girl whose parents decided, in the midst of their own grief, to help others. Max went on to campaign for an opt-out system, and when the Organ Donation (Deemed Consent) Act—which had cross-party support—received Royal Assent in March 2019 it became known as Max and Keira’s law. It came into force in England in May 2020.

Turn 167 · Speaker unlabelled

Max and Keira’s law was passed in the hope that more families would say yes and that fewer people like Max would be left waiting. More than six years later, the figures are sobering. I will not repeat the statistics quoted by the noble Lord, Lord Scriven, which are in front of me. Each patient who dies is someone’s child, parent or friend. There is also the figure quoted by the noble Lord that gives us some hope: almost 90% of families supported donation when they knew the person had recorded their wishes. It is the gap between the public support for donation in principle and families’ consent at the bedside where the problem lies. It is a gap that we surely must close.
