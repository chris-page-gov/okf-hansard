Turn 9 · Speaker unlabelled

Fifthly, on overseas providers, paragraph 4 confirms that a provider based anywhere in the world can register. Paragraph 60 refers authorities to Chapter 5 of the UK GDPR, now with the Act’s looser “not materially lower” test. Authoritative UK identity data could be held abroad. Authorities need clearer guidance on this, and Parliament deserves to know how many registered services process data outside the UK.

Turn 13 · Speaker unlabelled

Seventhly, on inclusion and redress, the code says nothing about people who cannot or will not use a digital service. The Minister told us in Committee that digital verification would never be mandatory. That promise needs keeping in practice, as these services spread into right to work, tenancy and age checks. On redress, the only route set out is the controller’s complaints process under Section 164A.

Turn 14 · Speaker unlabelled

Eighthly, on a level playing field with the state’s own wallet, earlier this year the Government said that the GOV.UK Wallet would itself be certified as a digital verification service. If so, it will both hold the credentials and compete with the providers who need them. Will private providers get the same verifiable credentials, such as a mobile driving licence, or only a derived and weaker version? Paragraph 69 is the right principle. The Government must apply it to themselves.

Turn 15 · Speaker unlabelled

Lastly, a practical point: the Explanatory Memorandum says that no impact assessment is needed because there are no new burdens. However, it also admits that the costs of digitising paper-only records, such as older birth certificates, were never counted and that the non-statutory guidance and templates will not be ready when the power is commenced. Authorities are being asked to open the gate before the manual arrives.
