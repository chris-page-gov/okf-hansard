Turn 25 · Speaker unlabelled

Amendment 131, in the name of my noble friends Lord Howe, Lord Kamall and Lord Markham, would require an electronic log to be taken on every occasion that information in the SPR is accessed. It would record who accessed it, from which organisation and in what role, when they did so, what they looked at and why. The main purpose of this is to build confidence in the system. People are worried about unauthorised access to their medical details, even though it is and will remain legal. They are worried with considerable justification, bearing in mind recent egregious examples of data breaches. A system with transparency built into it will show that there is no hiding place in that regard. Patients could inspect those entries free of charge and request an investigation where they believe that access was unauthorised or improper. Turn 26 · Speaker unlabelled

In Committee in the other place, the Minister described a roles-based access model, with near real-time monitoring to detect unusual patterns of access. She told the Committee that permission to view a record would not mean that a clinician was authorised to do so without a clinical need. At Second Reading, Ministers promised audit trails. We welcome all that but, if the Government intend to log every access, why can we not say so in the Bill? If that log exists, surely the patient—the person whose data it is—should be able to see it.

Turn 27 · Speaker unlabelled

Amendment 135, in the name of my noble friend Lord Howe, speaks to a similar theme. It is a way, I hope, for us to understand how the Government will approach particularly sensitive personal information. We appreciate that some medical history has far wider ramifications than the rest. Sexual and reproductive help, termination of a pregnancy, fertility treatment, a mental health diagnosis, treatment for substance misuse or genetic information can bear on a person’s relationships, employment and standing in their community. A patient may quite reasonably want greater control over who sees such data. That is entirely right.

Turn 28 · Speaker unlabelled

The amendment would therefore provide that such information is made available only to those whom the patient has explicitly authorised, whether named individuals or descriptions of people such as their GP or mental health team. Patients could give, vary or withdraw that consent easily and without charge. They could limit it to particular information, purposes or periods, and see a record of each time it is accessed. No patient could be refused care or be disadvantaged for declining to give consent. We are not naive about the clinical risks. A doctor in A&E may need to know about medication or a condition to treat someone safely. That is why the amendment contains a clear exception where access is necessary to prevent death or serious harm, where consent cannot practicably be obtained and where access is limited to what is strictly necessary.

Turn 31 · Speaker unlabelled

Many other amendments in this group speak to the same concerns on opting out of the common-law duty of confidentiality, on independent audits and on penalties for those who access records without authorisation. When we have a single patient record of the kind being designed, we need assurances. More importantly, patients need assurances that their data will be protected and kept confidential, and that the trust they place in the system will be repaid with the responsible governance of data that is personal in every sense of the word.
