Turn 19 · Speaker unlabelled

I turn to my amendments. I must declare a personal interest, which some noble Lords will know. My nephew, Myles Scriven, had a learning disability and died in the spring of 2023 from NHS neglect. He was 31 years old. In late 2022, Myles developed a blood clot and became short of breath. A consultant haematologist advised that his blood-thinning medication should be changed. It was not. His medical consultant assumed instead that Myles was not taking his tablets, despite being told on 10 occasions by Myles that he was taking them. He was discharged from hospital on the same drugs that were not dealing with the underlying causes of his clots.

Turn 20 · Speaker unlabelled

In March 2023, Myles’s breathing problems returned. He was offered a telephone consultation by a GP— a young man with real learning difficulties and difficulties communicating offered a phone call. He was not sent to A&E. He was seen three days later and again not referred, and no reasonable adjustments were made by his GP. Three weeks after that, he collapsed and died of a pulmonary embolism. The family had told professionals that he had learning disabilities and would need help with decisions. My husband David and I were his named advocates. Nobody called us. The coroner found that a lack of reasonable adjustments and neglect contributed to Myles’s death and that Myles could have been saved on at least three occasions.

Turn 21 · Speaker unlabelled

I tell the Committee this not for sympathy, but because every part of it—the diagnostic overshadowing, the assumptions that Myles was not taking his tablets, the missing reasonable adjustments, the family who were not heard—turns up again and again in the national reviews of these deaths. Myles is one of thousands. The latest LeDeR report, published this July, found that the median age at death for adults with learning disabilities was 62.8 years, against 81.8 for everyone else—a gap of 19 years. More than half died before 65. Some 39% of those deaths were avoidable; that is nearly double the general rate. The leading avoidable causes are pneumonia, heart disease and epilepsy, which the NHS treats every day for the rest of us.

Turn 22 · Speaker unlabelled

Why have I tabled these two amendments? The first reason is that there is no plan. It is 17 years since the last designated strategy for people with a learning disability. Since then, there have been programmes and pilots, with no health strategy setting out the standard of care that people with a learning disability can expect, the staff who are required to deliver services and who answers when good services are not delivered. What we get depends on where you live, and the life expectancy gap remains.

Turn 23 · Speaker unlabelled

Amendment 221 would require the Secretary of State to publish a health strategy within 12 months, built on a modern service framework—the Government’s own chosen tool—so that learning disability is not left off the list. It would require measurable outcomes and an annual dataset, nationally and locally, and a named board member in every ICB. It would also—and this is really important—require a co-national director who has a learning disability, because “nothing about us without us” has to mean something in the room where decisions are being made.
