Turn 25 · Speaker unlabelled

Some of this is welcome, but it does not add up to a plan. The letter tells us that 5 million people have completed the first part of the training. That counts who sat on a course; it says nothing about whether anyone lives longer. It cites an ambition of 75% for annual health checks, yet the same letter says that 79.8% were done last year, so the ambition is lower than what is already happening. It says that the NHS has repeatedly told services not to misuse DNACPR notices; it has had to do this repeatedly, because it is not happening. Much of what the letter relies on is run by NHS England, which the Bill abolishes. Nowhere does it say how, or by when, the 19-year life expectancy gap will close. Amendment 221 would make the Government answer that.

Turn 26 · Speaker unlabelled

The second reason is in Amendment 239. For a decade, LeDeR has told us why people are dying, but it has never been able to make anyone change. Its recommendations are advice; an ICB can receive and file them, and nothing in law requires it to act. That is why the same failures come back in report after report: the Government have become good at knowing why people die but remain poor at doing anything about it. Their position is now weaker. LeDeR has never been statutory, and Ministers have told this House that there are no plans to change that. This summer’s report was the last in its current form. Its host, NHS England, is being abolished by the Bill, and ICBs, which will have to do a new database, are being told to halve their running costs and staff.

Turn 28 · Speaker unlabelled

I anticipate that the Minister will tell us that the Government remain committed, that ICBs already have executive leads and, in the words of yesterday’s letter, that local panels review deaths to identify lessons. Indeed, they do, and then too often, nothing changes. The Government’s answer to the end of LeDeR in its current form is a new dataset. The dataset counts the dead; it will not make anyone keep the next person alive. I do not doubt the personal commitment of the Minister, but commitments that are not in statute rarely survive a reorganisation, and we are in the middle of the largest one in a generation.

Turn 29 · Speaker unlabelled

When Myles died, I said that his death must be a watershed. I meant that the next young person with a learning disability who is short of breath should be seen, believed and sent to a hospital, and, when that does not happen, someone is required in law to put it right and be held to account. These amendments ask for a plan and for the legal force to make sure that lessons from deaths are not just learned but acted upon. We owe Myles, and the thousands like him, better than the status quo. I beg to move.
