Turn 35 · Ben Obese-Jecty (Huntingdon) (Con)

I thank the Prime Minister for bringing this issue to the fore, and I thank Members on both sides of the House for their dogged determination in raising it and driving us to this point. As a veteran, I recognise that the Ministry of Defence does not have an unblemished record in how it has treated its service personnel in the past. Has the Prime Minister made an initial estimation of the scale of this investigation, such as the number of nuclear test veterans it affects, all the way through to their children, grandchildren and likely great-grandchildren? Has he also made an assessment of where any potential compensation pot would be paid from? Would it come from the Ministry of Defence or the Treasury?

Turn 36 · The Prime Minister

It is a sad thing to say, but I think there are 1,000 to 2,000 veterans still with us. I may have got that number wrong, but I think the figure is somewhere in that space. The descendants are many more, and as I said in my statement, health complications have carried on down the years. To me, it is not a question at this stage of who pays; we just have to do the right thing from here. It is important that we put those figures into the public domain as soon as we can, so that everyone can see the scale of what is involved here.

Turn 37 · Patricia Ferguson (Glasgow West) (Lab)

I thank the Prime Minister for his statement. I also thank the late Ken McGinley, who was a co-founder of the British Nuclear Test Veterans Association. Can the Prime Minister confirm that the inquiry will also look at the situation of those merchant mariners who were in the vicinity of the tests? They clearly recall being exposed to what seems, in retrospect, to have been radiation, yet their deployment records suggest that this was not the case. For many years, many of them have tried to get this information clarified, but they have come up against a brick wall. It would be very helpful if they could find out one way or another.

Turn 40 · The Prime Minister

I do believe that, like the Hillsborough panel, this should be a disclosure process, so that documents that have been held in secret can finally be brought into the public domain, when it is acceptable to do so. I think it is important for people to see how this was handled, and why we are in a position where, only today, we are setting a course towards the truth being known. In relation to the hon. Gentleman’s other point, there will be an interim report by 3 October. That is a challenging timetable, but I think it is an important one to set.
