Turn 121 · Speaker unlabelled

It is a slightly awkward situation. Does the Minister have any thoughts on this? I have a number of Labour friends who are teachers—maybe of the blue variety but, none the less, Labour Party. They go out canvassing. It is a separate activity, but they will be teaching people who are newly enfranchised. Will it not be tempting? You might think that they will know where the boundaries are, but we must consider this. Do we need to make it clear that this should never occur? One might think, “Oh well, you know the Labour Party”. But imagine a situation where teachers who are Green Party members of the “Zionism is racism” variety. If they started relating to pupils as would-be voters, think how uncomfortable that would be for Jewish pupils: the dangers of sectarianism entering the classroom that have just been mentioned. It is right that we think about this.

Turn 122 · Speaker unlabelled

The idea about age-appropriate election materials is important too, from a safeguarding point of view. Every teacher must always think, “Is this appropriate for this age group?” You do not teach people who are 13 in the same way that you teach someone who is 17. My concern is slightly different from that of the noble Lord, Lord Fuller. I do not want politics to be dumbed down, either, so how do you do it so you are not saying, “This is specifically designed for you young voters” and then it ends up having any depth gutted from it? These are things I want us to consider.

Turn 124 · Speaker unlabelled

I will give an example of a government-backed, specially designed piece of educational material that was designed to encourage and teach young people how to make the right choices—which always makes me anxious. It became an infamous example. It was a Home Office funded game for teenagers called “Pathways”: an educational interactive game for 11 to 18 year-olds. Teachers were urged by the Government to use it in the classroom. In terms of making the right choices, players were given control. They were asked to be a character called Charlie—in brackets, “they/them”. I kid you not. They had to negotiate scenarios involving online political content. Charlie was then rated depending on the choices that Charlie made. The rating was in relation to extremism or radicalisation.

Turn 126 · Speaker unlabelled

This project was widely and even internationally ridiculed. The Home Office, as noble Lords can imagine, has gone very quiet. It was ridiculed as manipulative propaganda. Ironically, it created a baddie, Amelia, a purple-haired, Goth-style cartoon character: a teenage girl in a mini union jack, from Bridlington, who tried to lure Charlie—they/them—on an anti-small boats demo. Now Amelia has become an international superstar: a meme all over the world. I am sure that is not what the Government were hoping would happen, but it does show you something about unintended consequences.

Turn 128 · Speaker unlabelled

One thing I want to stress to the Minister as we go forward with the Bill is that she sees tabling these probing amendments as an attempt to undermine a manifesto promise, destroy the Bill and all sorts of negative things.
