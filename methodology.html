A week in Parliament

Methodology and prompts

This pilot makes the official record of Parliament easier to follow across sittings. Each link in the graph connects a contribution to a recorded speaker or to a person, office, organisation, place, piece of legislation or other entity found in its text.

What went into the bundle

The first release covered 3,195 text-bearing passages published for the House of Commons and House of Lords on 16 and 17 November 2026. The coverage record lists the verified sittings in the latest release. The build counts published section trees and API items, includes statements and procedural items where Hansard supplies text, excludes layout markers and records divisions separately. How the links were made

  1. Identify the source. Preserve the House, sitting, section and contribution identifiers. Each assertion carries the source field and a digest of the retrieved data. Text matches also carry offsets into the normalised text so the evidence can be checked.
  2. Match names and relationships. Combine Parliament’s Member and Bill identifiers and recorded office titles with a curated list of names and aliases. Apply deterministic matching rules, preferring the longest unambiguous match. Keep a person, a constituency, an office and the dates of an appointment as distinct records.
  3. Trace earlier mentions. Use sitting calendars to fetch historical records. Index matching references, digests and text spans, discarding the fetched historical responses after analysis. Treat an earlier date, or an earlier position in the same section, as evidence of order. Contributions in separate sections on the same day have uncertain order.
  4. Package the evidence. Publish a browsable JSON bundle, a full YAML-LD graph and a companion with worked examples. When the Explorer bundle reaches its size limit, it keeps the week’s contributions and samples the earlier records. The local history extension covers 5,596 calendar-listed House sittings between 8 November 2007 and 29 October 2026. The initial capture and targeted searches cover more recent material.

A shared-name link records where the matching rule found a name or alias. Read the surrounding Hansard debate to establish how the contributions relate. Indirect references and aliases outside the vocabulary can be missed. The historical date range describes the material checked, and the vocabulary determines what can be found within it.

The design drew on Parliament’s controlled vocabulary, the European Parliament’s EPVOC and EuroVoc, Ireland’s use of Akoma Ntoso, the ParlaMint corpus and Canada’s LiPaD. International comparison · Domain and vocabulary profiles
